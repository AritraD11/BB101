DNA as Code — TA Handbook

BB 101 · Tutorial 2 · Batch T1 · LT 206

The script

Most of this room does not want to be here for biology. That's fine — don't sell it as biology. Sell it as a storage-and-processing system built out of molecules instead of silicon, because that's literally what it is, and it's the framing that runs through every slide below. Read the "Say this" blocks near-verbatim the first time you teach it, then make them your own.

The one framing to hold onto

DNA is a read-only archive. RNA is a disposable working copy. Protein is the compiled, running program. Every slide in this deck is a different piece of that pipeline — how the archive is physically built, how it gets copied, how a working copy gets decoded, and how scientists learned to hijack the copier. If a student is lost, drag them back to this one sentence.

Slide 1

How Nature Stores Information — DNA as Code

ON SCREEN: title + 5 topic tiles (DNA as Code · DNA & RNA Structure · Replication & Transcription · The Genetic Code & Wobble · PCR Amplification)
Say this

"Forget 'biology' for a second. What we're actually looking at today is a storage-and-processing system — nature's version of a hard drive, a compiler, and a photocopier, except it's built out of molecules instead of silicon. Every cell in your body is running the same four-letter code: A, T, G, C. That's it — no more symbols than that. And out of just four letters, that code builds everything about you.

Today we'll go through it exactly like you'd learn a new file format: what the data actually looks like at the byte level (structure), how the system copies itself without corrupting the original (replication) and exports a working copy to actually use (transcription), how that working copy gets decoded into a finished product — including a neat trick called 'wobble' that makes the decoding error-tolerant — and finally, how scientists hijacked this copying machinery to build the most powerful copy-paste command that exists: PCR, the same reaction behind every COVID test you've taken."

Ask the room

"Quick show of hands — who's taken a COVID PCR test?" (Most hands go up.) "Good — by the end of this hour you'll know exactly what that machine was doing to your swab."

Keep this slide under 3 minutes. It's a hook, not content — the temptation is to over-explain the roadmap before anyone has context for it.

Slide 2

Structure of Nucleotides

ON SCREEN: nucleotide = phosphate + sugar + base; nucleoside = sugar + base; phosphate types NMP / NDP / NTP
Say this

"If DNA is a long string of code, a nucleotide is one character in that string — except each 'character' is built from three physical pieces bolted together. There's the base, the actual letter, A/T/G/C — the informational part. There's a sugar ring, the physical scaffold holding the letter in place. And there's a phosphate group, the connector that links one sugar-base unit to the next — the same way a linked-list node has a pointer to the next node. Chain enough of these through their phosphate connectors and you get a strand of DNA.

One term worth locking in: sugar plus base, with no phosphate connector yet, is a nucleoside — a letter that hasn't been wired into the chain. Add the phosphate and it becomes a nucleotide, ready to link in. Notice phosphates come in mono-, di-, and triphosphate flavours — NMP, NDP, NTP. That triphosphate form matters later: it's not just a connector, it's carrying spare energy. Snapping off two of the three phosphates is literally what powers the 'write' step when a new letter gets added to a growing strand — you'll see this again in replication and PCR."

Ask the room

"If a nucleoside has no phosphate, can it link into a chain on its own?" (No — it needs the phosphate connector first.)

If they ask

"Isn't ATP the energy molecule — is that the same A as in DNA?" → Yes, literally the same base, Adenine. Nucleotides moonlight as the cell's energy currency and as signalling molecules too. Full answer in the Doubt Bank.

Slide 3

Structure of Nitrogenous Bases

ON SCREEN: purines A, G (double-ring) vs pyrimidines C, T, U (single-ring); pairing rules; U replaces T in RNA
Say this

"Four letters (five, counting RNA's swap), and they come in two physical sizes. Adenine and Guanine are purines — two fused rings, the 'big' letters. Cytosine, Thymine and Uracil are pyrimidines — one ring, the 'small' letters. Here's the rule that makes the whole double helix possible: a big letter always pairs with a small letter, never big-with-big or small-with-small. That's not arbitrary — it's what keeps the ladder's rungs exactly the same width all the way up, the same way a connector has to be a fixed size regardless of which two devices you're plugging together.

It's not just size-matching either — it's a specific lock-and-key: Adenine only pairs with Thymine (or Uracil in RNA), Cytosine only pairs with Guanine. Small hydrogen bonds — think of them as clips — hold each pair together: two clips for A-T, three clips for G-C. Remember that number. It comes back directly when we get to PCR's temperatures."

Ask the room

"Which pair do you think is harder to pull apart — A-T or G-C, and why?" (G-C — three hydrogen bonds vs two.)

If they ask

"Why does RNA swap Thymine for Uracil at all?" → Short answer: Thymine acts partly as a DNA damage-detection tag, which a disposable molecule like RNA doesn't need. Full answer in the Doubt Bank.

Slide 4

DNA & RNA: Structure of the Code

ON SCREEN: comparison table — strandedness, length, sugar, bases, pairing, stability, location/function
Say this

"Put the two molecules side by side — every difference here is a deliberate trade-off for the job each one does, not a random variation. DNA is the master copy: double-stranded, extremely stable, kept in the nucleus, not meant to move. Think of it as a read-only archive — the main branch nobody commits to directly. RNA is the working copy: single-stranded, less stable, made on demand and sent wherever it's needed — the temp file you generate from the archive so you can actually use the data without risking the original.

The chemistry backs this up: DNA's sugar is deoxyribose — 'ribose minus an oxygen' — and that missing -OH group removes a weak point in the backbone, making DNA chemically inert, exactly what you want for decades of storage. RNA's ribose keeps that -OH, which makes it more reactive and shorter-lived — fine, even useful, for something meant to be temporary."

Ask the room

"If DNA is so much more stable, why doesn't the cell just skip RNA and build proteins directly off DNA?" (Because that constantly exposes the one master copy to the busy machinery of the cell. RNA is a disposable proxy so the original is never directly at risk — full answer in the Doubt Bank.)

Slide 5

Replication & Transcription: Two Ways to Copy

ON SCREEN: DNA→DNA (semiconservative, leading/lagging strand, Okazaki fragments + ligase); DNA→RNA (transcription → mRNA transcript)
Say this

"Two completely different copy operations, worth keeping straight before the terms blur. DNA→DNA is replication: a full, faithful duplicate of the whole genome before a cell divides, so each daughter cell gets its own complete archive. DNA→RNA is transcription: copying out just one gene — one file — into a disposable working copy, without touching the rest of the archive.

Replication is called semiconservative, because every new double helix is a hybrid — one strand old, one strand new. Nothing is built entirely from scratch; you always keep half the original as scaffold. Here's the wrinkle: the enzyme building the new strand, DNA polymerase, can only extend in one direction — 5' to 3' — and can only read a template running the opposite way. Since the two original strands run opposite directions to each other, one new strand builds smoothly in one long pass — the leading strand — but the other has to be built backwards in short bursts, because the polymerase keeps jumping back to the fork and restarting — those bursts are Okazaki fragments, and ligase staples them into one continuous strand afterwards. Like a printer that can only feed paper one way: printing with the grain is one clean pass, printing against it means short strips taped together.

Then transcription: instead of duplicating everything, the cell zooms in on one gene, unzips just that stretch, and RNA polymerase reads the template and builds a matching RNA strand — same pairing rules, except A now pairs with Uracil. That RNA copy, the mRNA transcript, physically leaves for the ribosome, where it actually gets used."

Ask the room

"Which strand finishes faster — leading or lagging, and why?" (Leading — one continuous pass; lagging is start-stop fragments that then need stitching.)

If they ask

"How does DNA polymerase even start — doesn't it need something already there to extend?" → Yes. Flag it now and park it: this exact requirement is why PCR needs primers too, and you'll return to it in the PCR section.

Slide 6

Codons, the Central Dogma & the Wobble Position

ON SCREEN: DNA template → mRNA codon → tRNA anticodon → protein (worked example TAC → AUG → UAC → Met); degeneracy table; wobble (G·U) example
Say this

"This is the payoff slide — where 'code' stops being a metaphor and becomes literal. Follow one three-letter chunk of DNA all the way to a finished protein. The DNA template reads 3'-TAC-5'. Transcription copies that to an mRNA codon, 5'-AUG-3'. That three-letter codon is the actual unit the cell reads — not letter by letter, three at a time, like a fixed-width instruction. AUG means two things at once: 'start reading here' and 'the first amino acid is Methionine.' A matching tRNA shows up carrying the complementary three letters — the anticodon, 3'-UAC-5' — and delivered on its back is the actual building block, Methionine. The ribosome bonds it onto the growing chain, moves to the next codon, repeats. A few hundred times later: a folded, functional protein.

Now the interesting part: 64 possible three-letter codons, only 20 amino acids to encode — so most amino acids are named by more than one codon. Leucine has six. That's not sloppy design, that's redundancy on purpose — the same idea as a parity bit: if a mutation flips the third letter of a codon, there's a decent chance it still spells the same amino acid, so the mutation does nothing at all. And here's why the third letter specifically is the forgiving one: codon-anticodon pairing is strict at positions 1 and 2, but position 3 tolerates a looser match — including pairings that break the normal rule, like a G on the tRNA pairing with a U on the mRNA. That's the wobble position. Payoff: because that match doesn't have to be exact, one tRNA can read more than one codon, so the cell gets away with noticeably fewer than 61 tRNA types."

Ask the room

"If a mutation changes the third letter of a codon and the amino acid stays exactly the same, does that count as a mutation?" (Technically yes — a silent mutation: the DNA changed, but the protein produced didn't.)

If they ask

"What if a mutation DOES change the amino acid, or inserts an extra letter?" → Bridge to the Doubt Bank: missense (one amino acid changes), nonsense (an early stop codon truncates the protein), frameshift (an insertion/deletion not a multiple of three corrupts every codon downstream — like deleting one character from a script written in fixed-width instructions).

Slide 7

PCR: The Polymerase Chain Reaction

ON SCREEN: 3-step cycle (Denaturation ~95°C, Annealing 50–65°C, Extension ~72°C) × 25–35 cycles; copies ≈ 2ⁿ; 35 cycles ≈ 3.4×10¹⁰ copies
Say this

"Last stop, and this one you've personally lived through. PCR is scientists taking the cell's own copying machinery, pulling it out of the cell, and running it in a test tube entirely under their control, fast, over and over. Three steps, one cycle: Denaturation — heat to about 95°C, hot enough to snap every hydrogen bond from slide 3, splitting the double strand into two singles. Annealing — cool to 50–65°C, and short synthetic primers find and stick to the specific stretch you want copied, marking exactly where to start. Extension — heat back to 72°C, and Taq polymerase extends new strands from those primers, same 5'-to-3' addition as replication.

Then — the entire trick — you don't do that once. You loop it. Repeat 25 to 35 times, and because every molecule made in one cycle becomes a template for the next, the copy count doesn't add, it doubles: after n cycles, roughly two-to-the-n copies. Run it 35 times and one molecule becomes about thirty-four billion. That's a for-loop that doubles its output every iteration — same curve as compound interest, same curve as Moore's Law, just applied to molecules.

One detail that makes this actually work: Taq polymerase comes from a bacterium, Thermus aquaticus, discovered living in geothermal hot springs. A normal enzyme would cook and fall apart the moment you hit 95°C — Taq doesn't, so it survives all 35 denaturation steps without needing to be topped up. Before Taq, this meant manually adding fresh enzyme after every single heating cycle. And the payoff you've actually lived: your COVID nasal-swab test worked exactly this way. There usually isn't nearly enough viral genetic material in a swab to detect directly — so the test doubles it, and doubles it again, thirty-something times, until a handful of starting molecules becomes billions, enough to light up a detector. You weren't testing for the virus directly — you were testing whether this doubling loop found anything worth doubling."

Ask the room

"If PCR starts from one molecule and doubles every cycle, how many cycles to beat a million copies?" (2²⁰ ≈ 1.05 million — so 20 cycles.)

If they ask

"COVID's genome is RNA though — how does a DNA-copying reaction work on that?" → An extra enzyme, reverse transcriptase, first back-copies the RNA into DNA (the "RT" in RT-PCR), and standard PCR takes over from there. Full answer in the Doubt Bank.

DNA as code

The framing that makes everything else click for a room that thinks in systems, not organisms. Every biology term in this tutorial has a direct computing equivalent — not a loose metaphor, a genuinely structural one.

The pipeline

DNA (archive) → RNA (working copy) → Protein (running program). Three stages, three physical molecules, one direction of information flow — Francis Crick named this the Central Dogma in 1958. Nearly everything in this tutorial is detail on one of these three arrows.

Term translator

A single gene→one file / one function
RNA polymerase (transcription)→export / copy command
mRNA→compiled bytecode, sent off to run
Ribosome→processor / interpreter
tRNA→lookup-table adapter
Codon (3 letters)→fixed-width instruction word
Protein→compiled, running executable
Mutation→bit flip

Frameshift mutation→a deleted byte corrupting every instruction after it

PCR cycle→one loop iteration, output doubles

Reuse this table shamelessly. Every time a student's eyes glaze at a biology term, point at its row.

Why this analogy actually holds up

It's not just a teaching trick — DNA genuinely is a digital storage medium in the strict sense: a sequence built from a small, discrete alphabet (A/T/G/C — effectively 2 bits per letter), read by machinery that doesn't care what the sequence "means," only what it says. Researchers are actively building real archival data-storage systems out of synthetic DNA today, because the information density is extraordinary — current lab demonstrations pack on the order of hundreds of petabytes into a single gram, several orders of magnitude denser than any silicon medium. When you tell this room "DNA is a storage format," you are not simplifying for effect. You are stating it plainly.

Don't overclaim

The Central Dogma describes the dominant flow of information, not an absolute law. Retroviruses like HIV run it backwards — RNA back into DNA, via an enzyme called reverse transcriptase — which is exactly the borrowed trick behind RT-PCR (see the PCR tab). Mention this once, in passing; it's a good "even the rule has a well-understood exception" moment for a skeptical room, and it sets up the COVID-test callback perfectly.

Phosphate group flavours

Phosphates don't just show up once — they come in mono-, di-, and triphosphate forms, and the difference matters:

FormShort forRole
Monophosphate (NMP)e.g. AMPOne phosphate — the minimum needed to link into a chain.
Diphosphate (NDP)e.g. ADPTwo phosphates — an intermediate energy state.
Triphosphate (NTP)e.g. ATP, dNTPsThree phosphates — the form DNA/RNA polymerase actually uses as raw material. Breaking off two phosphates releases the energy that pays for adding the letter to the chain.
Say it like this

"Every single letter added to a growing DNA or RNA strand arrives as a triphosphate — a dNTP. The cell isn't just fetching a letter, it's fetching a pre-paid letter: two of its three phosphates get sacrificed on the spot to power the bond that welds it into the chain. That's not a coincidence — it's the same molecule family as ATP, the cell's general-purpose energy currency, doing double duty as both a genetic letter and a battery."

Pyrimidines — single ring, "small"

C Cytosine    T Thymine    U Uracil

One ring. Uracil replaces Thymine in RNA only — same shape, one fewer methyl group.

Pairing rules

Why purine-pyrimidine is mandatory

Pair two purines and the rung is too wide; pair two pyrimidines and it's too narrow. Obligate purine-with-pyrimidine pairing is what keeps the helix's diameter perfectly constant along its entire length — the molecular equivalent of a connector that has to be a fixed size no matter which two devices are on either end.

Plant this now, pay it off in PCR

G-C pairs grip harder (3 bonds) than A-T pairs (2 bonds). That means GC-rich DNA needs more heat to melt apart than AT-rich DNA — which is exactly why PCR's denaturation and annealing temperatures aren't arbitrary numbers. Flag it here; the payoff lands in the PCR tab.

Copying the code

Slide 5. Two different copy operations that get confused constantly: replication duplicates the whole archive; transcription exports one file. Step through each.

Replication: DNA → DNA

Semiconservative — every new duplex keeps exactly one original strand. Six stages, tap through them.

The directionality problem

DNA polymerase can only extend an existing strand 5'→3', reading the template 3'→5'. Since the two template strands run in opposite directions, one new strand is built in one continuous pass (leading), and the other has to be built backwards in short bursts, restarted repeatedly from the fork (lagging — Okazaki fragments), later sealed into one strand by ligase.

Transcription: DNA → RNA

Copies out one gene only, not the whole genome. Four stages.

Say it like this

"Replication is a full backup before the system forks into two. Transcription is grep-ing one function out of a huge codebase into a small working file, without ever touching the original repository. Different scale, different purpose, same underlying base-pairing rules doing the copying."

Say it like this

"This is a parity bit, not sloppiness. If a random mutation flips one letter and the codon still names the same amino acid, the mutation is invisible — the protein comes out identical. Redundancy in the encoding buys tolerance against noise, exactly like adding a checksum."

PCR: the exponential copy-paste

Slide 7. The reaction behind every COVID nasal swab, forensic DNA match, and sequencing prep step — and it's really just a for-loop that doubles.

"This is copies = 1; for i in range(n): copies *= 2. Nothing more exotic than that. Run it 35 times and one molecule becomes about thirty-four billion — same exponential curve as compound interest, same curve as Moore's Law, just running on molecules instead of money or transistors."

Why Taq mattered

Before Taq was discovered (isolated 1976), PCR used an enzyme that died at 95°C — meaning a scientist had to manually pipette in fresh enzyme after every single heating cycle, all night, for every sample. Taq's heat tolerance is the single fact that turned PCR from a manual lab chore into something you load into a machine (a thermocycler) and walk away from.

The callback — land the tutorial here

Your COVID nasal-swab test was RT-PCR. SARS-CoV-2's genome is RNA, not DNA, so an extra enzyme — reverse transcriptase — first back-copies it into DNA (the "RT"). Standard PCR then doubles that DNA copy 30-plus times, because a swab usually doesn't contain nearly enough viral material to detect directly. A "positive" result means the doubling loop found something worth doubling — not that anyone looked at an actual virus. This is the single most relatable fact in the whole tutorial. Use it to close.
