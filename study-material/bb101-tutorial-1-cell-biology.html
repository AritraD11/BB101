Run the tutorial

The slide deck has six slides and about four real ideas. This page holds those four ideas, the layer of background underneath them, and the questions students actually ask — so nothing lands as a surprise.

The through-line

Every slide answers one question: why is life built out of cells at all? The answer is size. A cell is small because chemistry has to reach the middle fast enough to keep it alive. Everything else — organelles, tissues, organs, specialised shapes — is what life does once it accepts that limit. If a student remembers only one thing, make it this.

If you get stuck

You are not expected to be a biologist, and the students know you are a TA. Three moves that always work:

  1. Convert the question into the principle. Most first-year questions are a special case of surface area, of membranes, or of structure-follows-function. Name which one it is, reason from there out loud.
  2. Say the boundary honestly. "That's past what this tutorial covers — it's in the lecture on membrane transport" is a completely respectable answer, and far better than a guess. Write it down and follow up next week.
  3. Turn it back to the room. "Good — what would you predict, and why?" Half the time a student answers it, and you learn how they are thinking.
Guard against

The three statements most likely to get you into trouble, because they are the ones textbooks oversimplify: "prokaryotes have no organelles" (they have ribosomes), "only plants have cell walls" (fungi and bacteria do too), and "plant cells don't need mitochondria" (they very much do). All three are unpacked in the Doubt Bank.

Organisation of life

Slide 2. Life is built in nested layers, each one made of the layer below it. The point of the ladder is not to memorise six words — it is to see where alive begins.

The line that matters

Molecules and organelles are not alive on their own. A ribosome cannot grow, respond, or reproduce itself. A cell can do all three. That is why the cell — and not the molecule, not the organelle — is called the smallest unit of life. Everything above the cell is life organised; everything below is life's parts.

Expect this

"Where do organ systems fit? The slide skips them." The full ladder is organ → organ system → organism. The heart is an organ; the heart plus blood vessels plus blood is the circulatory system. The slide compresses it — say so, don't pretend the gap isn't there.

"What about atoms — below molecules?" Yes: atoms → molecules → macromolecules → organelles. Biologists usually start at molecules because that is where the chemistry becomes specifically biological.

What is a cell?

Slide 3. The definition on the slide is correct but inert. Here is the definition plus the reasoning that makes it stick.

Definition on the slide

A cell is the smallest, basic unit of life that makes up all living things, acting as the structural and functional building block of all organisms.

Unpack the three claims in that sentence, because students will be examined on exactly these:

  • Structural unit — every organism is built out of cells, the way a wall is built out of bricks. Nothing living is made of anything else.
  • Functional unit — and unlike a brick, each cell independently does the things life does: takes in energy, builds molecules, responds to its surroundings, divides.
  • Smallest — cut a cell in half and you do not get two smaller living things. You get debris. This is the test of a "unit".

Earlier: Robert Hooke (1665) saw and named "cells" in cork — dead cell walls, not living cells. Antonie van Leeuwenhoek (1674) was the first to see living cells, his "animalcules". Hooke named it; Leeuwenhoek saw it alive. Students mix these two up constantly.

Why cells are small — the whole argument

A cell takes everything in and pushes everything out across its surface. But everything it must feed and clear is in its volume. Grow a cell and the volume outruns the surface, because volume scales with the cube of length and surface only with the square. Past a certain size the membrane simply cannot keep up.

Say it like this

"Double the cell's width. Its surface goes up 4×, but its volume goes up 8×. So every unit of surface now has twice as much cell to supply — and the trip to the middle takes four times as long, because diffusion time scales with distance squared. A cell doesn't stay small out of modesty. It stays small because it would suffocate."

How big are things, actually

Bar length is on a logarithmic scale — each equal step is a 10× jump. Worth saying out loud: a neuron running from your spinal cord to your toe is a single cell about a metre long. It gets away with it by being extremely thin, so nothing is ever far from its surface.

Expect this

"Then how are egg cells so big?" An ostrich egg yolk is a single cell about 8 cm across. It cheats: the bulk is stored, chemically inert yolk, not busy metabolising cytoplasm. The living, active layer is thin and near the surface. The rule holds for active cytoplasm.

Prokaryotic vs eukaryotic

Slide 4. The slide names four axes — size and complexity, organelles, nucleus, organisms. Take them in reverse order of importance so the nucleus lands last and hardest.

The one-line distinction

Eukaryote = "true nucleus" (Greek eu = true, karyon = kernel/nut). Prokaryote = "before the nucleus". Membrane-bound nucleus present or absent — that is the whole dividing line, and the etymology does the teaching for you.

The trap on this slide

"Prokaryotes have no organelles" is what the slide says and what students will write. It is half true: they have no membrane-bound organelles. They do have ribosomes, which are organelles — just non-membrane-bound ones. Always say the word "membrane-bound". If a student catches you, they are right, and you should say so.

Also

Prokaryote does not mean bacterium. There are two prokaryotic domains: Bacteria and Archaea. Archaea look like bacteria but their membrane lipids and gene-handling machinery are different — in several ways they are closer to us than to bacteria. The three domains are Bacteria, Archaea, Eukarya.

The question that will come

"Why is 50S + 30S = 70S and not 80S?" Because S is a Svedberg unit — a sedimentation coefficient, measuring how fast a particle settles in a centrifuge. It depends on mass and on shape and density, so it does not add. When the two subunits join, the assembled particle is more compact than the two apart, and sediments at 70S. This gets asked every single year. Have it ready.

Kill this myth on sight

"Plants have chloroplasts, so they don't need mitochondria." Wrong, and extremely common. Chloroplasts make glucose; mitochondria spend it to make ATP. A plant respires all day and all night, in every cell — including roots, which never see light and have no chloroplasts at all. Every plant cell has mitochondria.

Turgor — the demo you can do with words

The vacuole fills with water and pushes the cytoplasm hard against the cell wall. That outward push is turgor pressure, and it is what holds a leaf flat and a stem upright. Stop watering the plant, the vacuoles lose water, the push disappears — the plant wilts. Water it and it recovers, because the wall was still there holding the shape. Ask them why a wilted plant recovers but a crushed one doesn't.

Cells in our body

Slide 6. One idea, stated on the slide: cells differ in shape and size because each is specialised for a job. Run it as structure → function, every time.

The rule

Structure follows function. Do not present these as seven shapes to memorise. Present each shape as the answer to a design problem — then ask the room to guess the shape before you show it. "This cell has to carry oxygen and squeeze through vessels narrower than itself. What shape would you build?"

The closing question — plant it yourself

"If every cell in my body has the same DNA, why is a neuron not a fat cell?" Because they use different parts of the same DNA. Every cell carries the full genome, but each cell type switches on only the genes it needs and keeps the rest silent — this is differential gene expression. Same cookbook in every kitchen; each kitchen cooks a different page. This is the single best question to end the tutorial on, because it opens directly onto the rest of the course.

Counts

  • Cells in a human body: ~30–37 trillion (3.0–3.7 × 1013).
  • Bacterial cells living in and on you: roughly the same order — about 38 trillion. The old "10× more bacteria than human cells" line is outdated; the current estimate is close to 1:1.
  • Distinct human cell types: ~200+, depending on how you count.
  • Red blood cell lifespan ~120 days; gut lining ~5 days; most neurons a lifetime.
