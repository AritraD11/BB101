PCR — Study Guide

For you, not the class · read before writing Monday's script

Start here

You've had this exact reaction run on you — every COVID nasal swab was PCR. This page is for understanding it yourself first. No students, no performance, just get it straight in your own head before you build the script.

The one sentence to hold onto

PCR is a molecular photocopier: give it a test tube with a trace of DNA in it, tell it exactly which short stretch you want (with two short DNA "bookmarks" called primers), and it hands you back a billion identical copies of just that stretch — nothing else. Everything else on this page is detail underneath that one sentence.

What problem does it actually solve?

DNA analysis — reading a sequence, testing for a mutation, matching a crime-scene sample — needs a usable amount of DNA to work with. A drop of blood, a hair root, a nasal swab: these contain real DNA, but only a few trillionths of a gram of the specific stretch you care about. That's not enough material for any machine to reliably read. PCR's only job is to close that gap — take an unusable trace and turn it into a usable quantity, of one exact target region, without needing a living cell at all.

Before PCR existed

The only way to get more of a specific DNA fragment was to splice it into a living bacterial cell (a plasmid) and let the bacteria's own division machinery copy it as the colony grew — a process called cloning. It worked, but it took days, needed a working cell-culture lab, and gave you an amount you couldn't finely control.

PCR (invented by Kary Mullis in 1983, at Cetus Corporation — he won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry for it in 1993, one of the fastest bench-to-Nobel turnarounds ever) replaced all of that with a 2–3 hour reaction in a single tube, in a machine you load and walk away from. That speed and simplicity is the entire reason it's now the default first step in nearly every corner of biology, medicine, and forensics.

Keep this framing as you read on

Everything below is really one machine doing one repeating job. The three sections after this — the kit, the cycle, why it explodes — are just: what's in the tube, what the machine does to it, and why the result grows the way it does. Read them in order once, then use the Doubt Bank and Cheat Sheet as reference.

The kit

Seven things go into the tube. Each has exactly one job. Analogies below lean on circuits and systems, since that's the language you already think in.

1 · Template DNA

The sample — contains the target sequence somewhere inside it, even if it's a tiny trace.

Like: the source document you're photocopying. PCR never invents new sequence — it only exists if the template already contains it.
2 · Forward & reverse primers

Short (~18–25 base) synthetic DNA snippets. One binds each strand, flanking the target region on either side.

Like: setting a start address and an end address on a memory bus. Together they define exactly which window of the whole genome gets read out — and give the enzyme below a place it's allowed to start.
3 · Taq DNA polymerase

The enzyme that actually builds the new strand, extending from each primer.

Like: the write head. It can only extend an existing end — it cannot start a strand from nothing, same as an assembler that needs a starting cursor position before it can lay down anything.
4 · dNTPs

Deoxyribonucleotide triphosphates — the four DNA letters in loose, free-floating form: dATP, dTTP, dGTP, dCTP. Each carries its own energy-supplying phosphate tail.

Like: a parts bin the write head pulls from, one component at a time, choosing whichever one the template underneath calls for.
5 · Mg²⁺ (as MgCl₂)

A required cofactor — Taq polymerase's active site literally does not function without it.

Like: a bias voltage the circuit needs to be powered from. It's not part of the final DNA output, but nothing runs without it present.
6 · Buffer

Holds pH and ionic strength in the enzyme's working range for the whole reaction.

Like: a voltage regulator — keeps every other component operating inside its rated conditions instead of drifting.
7 · Water (nuclease-free)

Brings the reaction to its final working volume. Must be free of contaminating enzymes/DNA.

Like: a clean ground rail in a sensitive analog circuit — noise anywhere in it corrupts the whole result.
Why Taq specifically mattered

Ordinary enzymes fall apart (denature) around 40–50°C, let alone the ~95°C the first step of every cycle needs. Taq is sourced from Thermus aquaticus, a bacterium that naturally lives in geothermal hot springs at 70–80°C — so it's built to survive heat that would destroy a normal protein. Before Taq was identified and purified (1976, put to use in PCR by 1983), a researcher had to manually pipette in a fresh dose of enzyme after every single heating step, all night, for every sample. Taq's heat tolerance is the one fact that turned PCR from a manual chore into something you load into a machine and walk away from.

The 3-step cycle

One PCR "cycle" is nothing but three temperatures held in sequence for a set number of seconds each. The machine (a thermal cycler) is just a small, programmable heating/cooling block — no moving parts beyond that. Think of it as a 3-state clock-driven loop, repeated 25–35 times.

1
Denaturation
94–98 °C

Heat breaks the hydrogen bonds holding the two DNA strands together — the weak, sideways bonds, not the strong backbone. The double helix "unzips" into two separate single strands. Nothing is destroyed; it's a physical separation, fully reversible on cooling.

Why this temp: hot enough to overcome even G-C base pairs (3 H-bonds, stronger than A-T's 2).

2
Annealing
50–65 °C

Temperature drops enough for the short primers to find and grip their one exact complementary match among millions of possible positions on the template.

Why this temp: a Goldilocks window tied to the primer's own melting point — too hot and even the correct primer can't hold on; too cold and primers stick loosely in the wrong places too.

3
Extension
~72 °C

Taq polymerase attaches to each primer's free end and starts adding matching dNTPs one at a time (A↔T, G↔C), moving along the template — building a brand-new complementary strand.

Why this temp: Taq's own working optimum — fastest, most accurate synthesis happens right here.

Say it like this

"Denaturation opens the zip. Annealing places a bookmark on each side of the page you want. Extension photocopies from bookmark to bookmark. Then the whole thing repeats — and every copy made in one round becomes raw material for the next."

Why does the temperature need to cycle at all?

Because the three steps need physically opposite conditions, and no single temperature satisfies all three at once: denaturation needs the strands forced apart by heat; annealing needs them cool enough for a short primer to grip firmly; extension needs the mid-range temperature Taq actually works best at. You can't hold one setting and get all three — so the machine ramps through all three, every round, like a clock driving a 3-phase state machine.

A subtlety worth knowing (not essential, but it'll come up)

In the very first couple of cycles, Taq often runs past the far end of the template before the cycle ends, producing variable-length "long" products rather than a clean, exact-length fragment. It's only from roughly the third cycle onward that the true primer-to-primer-length product starts to dominate and take over the exponential growth. Doesn't change the outcome — after 30 cycles it's overwhelmingly the correct-length product — but it explains why cycle counts below ~3 don't cleanly follow the doubling formula in the next section.

Why it explodes

The name is "chain reaction" for a reason. This is the part of PCR that will feel most immediately familiar to you.

The core trick

It is not "add one copy per cycle." Every molecule present at the start of a cycle — the original template and every copy made so far — gets copied again during that same cycle. The copies become templates for the next round, which become templates for the round after that. That's compounding, not additive growth — the same shape as compound interest, or a binary counter doubling on every clock tick, or (the deck's own name for it) a chain reaction.

The formula

Nₙ = N₀ × 2ⁿ

N₀ = molecules you start with · n = number of cycles run · 2ⁿ = the ideal amplification factor.

The honest reality check

2ⁿ is an ideal, not what a real tube does forever. Somewhere around cycle 30–40, real reactions plateau: primers and dNTPs get physically used up (like a battery depleting), Taq's activity gradually declines from being heat-cycled dozens of times (thermal fatigue, in your terms), and at high product concentration the two complementary strands increasingly find and re-anneal to each other instead of waiting around for a primer — which stalls new priming. The curve you'd actually plot flattens out well before the formula does. This is exactly why real protocols don't just run 60 cycles "to be safe" — past the plateau, more cycles buys you almost nothing.

What it's actually used for

Same machine, same 3-step cycle, every time. What changes between applications is which primers you choose — which exact stretch of DNA you're asking it to copy — and what you do with the copies afterward. The crime-scene example below walks through that whole chain in enough detail to answer almost any follow-up question a student could ask about it.

The chain, end to end

A hair is found at a scene. The question "how does that become a DNA profile" hides several separate steps, and it matters that you can tell them apart: hair sample → biological cells → extracted DNA → template → target region selected → PCR amplification → DNA profile → comparison. PCR is only one link in that chain — the amplification step. Everything before it is sample preparation; everything after it is analysis. A common mixing-up point is worth flagging early:

PCR does not extract DNA

PCR amplifies DNA that has already been extracted and purified. Getting DNA out of the hair in the first place is separate lab chemistry — lysis, protein and membrane removal, purification — that happens before the tube ever sees a thermal cycler. If a student asks "does the PCR machine pull the DNA out of the sample," the answer is no: by the time anything goes into that machine, the DNA is already sitting there, isolated and ready.

Step 1 — what a hair actually gives you

Not every part of a hair is useful. The shaft is largely dead, keratinized material with little to no intact nuclear DNA. The useful source is the root — specifically the follicle tissue and root sheath cells, which are nucleated and still carry a full copy of the genome. This is why forensic collection protocols care whether a hair was pulled (root attached) versus shed or cut (root often missing): it directly determines whether there's a nucleus to extract DNA from at all.

Step 2 — extraction produces the template

1
Break open the cells

Lysis — chemically or mechanically rupturing the cell and nuclear membranes.

2
Release the DNA

Once membranes are broken, the DNA is free in solution, mixed in with proteins, membrane fragments, and other cellular debris.

3
Purify it

Everything that isn't DNA — proteins, lipids, salts — is removed, leaving a clean solution of DNA alone.

What comes out the far end is the template: not a separate substance, just the purified DNA itself, sitting ready to be copied. When your slide says "DNA template contains the target DNA sequence," it means exactly this — the DNA you pulled out of the hair is going to serve as the source PCR reads from.

Step 3 — choosing what to amplify

A single human cell's DNA runs to roughly three billion base pairs. PCR does not amplify all of it — one reaction copies one chosen region, typically a few hundred bases long. For forensic identification, that region is an STR locus (short tandem repeat): a short DNA motif that repeats back-to-back a variable number of times, where the repeat count differs enough from person to person to be useful for distinguishing individuals. Picture the extracted DNA as an unbroken line with one small stretch marked out as the target:

5′ ————————————————— [ TARGET REGION ] ————————————————— 3′

Real forensic panels amplify several STR loci from the same sample simultaneously (multiplexing) — more on that below.

Step 4 — the target region determines the primers, not the other way around

This is the piece most easily glossed over: primers are not generic reagents that go looking for a match. The sequence immediately flanking a chosen target — on both sides — is already known in advance, because the STR locus itself has already been mapped and characterized by prior research. A primer is designed, deliberately, to be the exact complement of one specific flanking sequence. Two are needed because the target sits between two flanks:

5′ ———AAAAA [ TARGET REGION ] TTTTT——— 3′ → forward primer binds here, matching AAAAA reverse primer ← binds here, matching TTTTT

Once mixed with the extracted DNA, a primer anneals only where its complementary sequence genuinely exists. If the sample's DNA does not contain that flanking sequence — wrong locus, degraded sample, contamination — the primer has nothing to bind, and no product forms. That is also, incidentally, why a failed or absent PCR result is itself informative: it tells you the target sequence was not present in usable form, not just that "something didn't work."

Restating what a primer is, from the Kit tab

A primer is a short single-stranded DNA sequence supplying the free 3′-OH end that DNA polymerase needs before it can extend anything. The primer doesn't just mark a location — it physically provides the starting point synthesis attaches to. No primer, no 3′-OH, no reaction.

Step 5 — why two primers, specifically

DNA is double-stranded and the two strands run in opposite directions (antiparallel). Amplifying the region means copying both strands, so each strand needs its own primer, and the two are read toward each other from opposite ends of the target:

Strand 1: 5′ ———————————————————————————— 3′ forward primer → [ TARGET ] ← reverse primer Strand 2: 3′ ———————————————————————————— 5′

Together, the two primers do double duty: they mark both boundaries of the region that gets copied, and each one hands DNA polymerase a 3′-OH to build from on its own strand. Neither primer alone could do both jobs.

Step 6 — inside the tube, tied back to the hair

1
Template + primers, mixed

The extracted DNA and the two locus-specific primers (plus Taq, dNTPs, Mg²⁺, buffer, water — the full kit from the previous tab) go into one tube.

2
Denaturation, 94–98 °C

Strands separate.

3
Annealing, 50–65 °C

The forward and reverse primers find and bind their exact flanking matches.

4
Extension, ~72 °C

Taq builds outward from each primer, across the target region.

5
Repeat 25–35 times

Millions of copies of just that STR locus accumulate — nothing else in the genome is amplified.

6
Fragment analysis

The amplified fragments are separated by size (capillary electrophoresis) and measured.

7
DNA profile

The pattern of fragment sizes across several loci, run together, is the profile — not any single locus on its own.

8
Comparison

The profile is checked against a suspect's. A match at every tested marker means the suspect's DNA is consistent with the sample.

Three roles that are easy to blur together

Extraction, primer design, and polymerase activity solve three different problems, in that order, and mixing them up is the single most common source of confusion in this whole topic:

Extraction

Gets DNA physically out of the biological sample. Happens once, before PCR starts.

Primers

Decide which region of that DNA gets copied. Chosen in advance, based on already-known flanking sequence.

DNA polymerase

Does the actual copying, cycle after cycle, extending from wherever the primers attached.

The whole chain as one analogy

Treat the entire human genome as a single enormous book. The hair sample hands you a physical copy of that book — extraction is what gets the book out of the evidence bag and onto the desk. You want one specific paragraph, not the whole text. The template is the book itself. The two primers are bookmarks, placed on either side of exactly the paragraph you want, based on already knowing which page it's on. Taq polymerase is the photocopier. Running PCR is running that photocopier repeatedly between the two bookmarks. What comes out is millions of copies of one paragraph — never the rest of the book.

One correction worth holding onto

The primer does not decide whose DNA it is. It decides which region gets copied — and it's the same primer pair for every person tested at that locus. What actually varies from person to person is the length or sequence within that amplified region (how many times an STR motif repeats, for instance). The primer is identical across everyone; the difference only shows up in what comes out the other end. This is the detail that makes DNA profiling work at all, and it's also the detail most likely to get flattened into "the primer matches your DNA" — which isn't quite it.

Your own COVID nasal swab (RT-PCR)

SARS-CoV-2's genome is RNA, not DNA, so there's one extra step first: an enzyme called reverse transcriptase back-copies the viral RNA into DNA (the "RT" in RT-PCR). From there, it's standard PCR — the same 3-step cycle doubles that DNA copy 30-plus times, because a swab usually doesn't contain nearly enough viral material to detect directly. A "positive" result means the amplification loop found something worth doubling — not that anyone looked at an actual virus under a microscope.

Two more, briefly

Paternity / relatedness testing

Exactly the same STR-profile idea as forensics — compare a child's amplified profile against a parent's at the same loci.

Genetic disease screening

Primers target the specific gene region of interest (e.g. a sickle-cell or cystic-fibrosis marker); the amplified product is then sequenced or checked for the mutation.

Genetic code & codons

Slides 10–12 shift topic — from making copies of DNA to reading what DNA says. Different half of the same deck, different question entirely: PCR asks "how do I get more of this sequence," the genetic code asks "what does this sequence actually mean."

Where this sits relative to PCR

DNA carries the instructions; a cell reads them in two further steps. Transcription copies a gene's DNA sequence into mRNA. Translation reads that mRNA, three bases at a time, and builds a protein one amino acid per triplet. PCR touches none of this — it only ever copies DNA. It doesn't transcribe, and it doesn't translate.

1
DNA

The archived instructions. PCR can amplify a chosen region of this, and stops there.

2
Transcription → mRNA

A working copy of one gene, made by an entirely different enzyme (RNA polymerase, not Taq).

3
Translation → Protein

The ribosome reads mRNA in codons and assembles the corresponding amino acid chain.

Asked directly: does PCR make protein? No. PCR's output is always more DNA, never RNA or protein — those are separate cellular machinery, running afterward, on a different molecule.

Why codons are 3 bases long, not 2 or 1

mRNA has four possible letters (A, U, G, C) and needs to specify 20 amino acids. A 1-base code gives 4 possible signals — nowhere near enough. A 2-base code gives 4² = 16 — still short of 20. A 3-base code gives 4³ = 64 — comfortably more than 20, with room to spare. Three is the smallest word length that covers the alphabet needed, which is exactly why the code reads in triplets.

A codon is a group of 3 mRNA bases. Each one specifies either one amino acid or "stop."

  • 64 possible codons total (4³) — 61 code for amino acids, 3 are stop signals (UAA, UAG, UGA).
  • AUG is the start codon and also codes for the amino acid Methionine.
  • Degenerate: most amino acids are specified by more than one codon (there are only 20 amino acids sharing 61 sense codons).
  • Unambiguous: even so, each individual codon only ever points to one specific amino acid — never two.
  • Nearly universal: the same codon table applies across almost all known life, with a handful of known exceptions in certain organelles.
Codon vs. anticodon, in one example

The codon lives on mRNA; the matching anticodon lives on a tRNA molecule and pairs against it directly. For the start codon mRNA 5′-AUG-3′, the tRNA carrying Methionine presents the anticodon 3′-UAC-5′ — read in the opposite direction, base-paired exactly the way two DNA strands pair. The ribosome holds codon and anticodon together just long enough to transfer that tRNA's amino acid onto the growing chain, then moves to the next codon.

The exercise on your slides — step by step

Given a protein Met–Ser–Arg–Trp–Lys–Stop, write an mRNA sequence that could produce it. This method works for any such problem, not just this one:

1
List the amino acids in order

Already given here: Met, Ser, Arg, Trp, Lys, Stop.

2
Look up every codon for each one

Met → AUG only. Ser → 6 options. Arg → 6 options. Trp → UGG only. Lys → 2 options. Stop → 3 options (any one ends it).

3
Pick one codon per amino acid

Any valid choice works — this is the whole point. Most amino acids have more than one legal codon, so there's no single "correct" answer, only valid ones.

4
String them together, in order, no gaps

That concatenated sequence is your mRNA answer.

5
Check it

Read your own answer back in groups of 3 from the start and confirm each triplet decodes, via the chart, back to Met–Ser–Arg–Trp–Lys–Stop in that exact order.

Mutations (slide 13)

The deck closes with one figure showing three ways a nucleotide sequence can change, and what each does to the resulting protein. This is where the triplet nature of the code from the last tab stops being an abstract fact and starts explaining something concrete: why some changes are minor and others rewrite everything downstream.

The baseline — a normal gene

DNA (template)

Met–Lys–Phe–Gly–Ala

Grouped in triplets: AUG·AAG·UUU·GGC·GCA → Met·Lys·Phe·Gly·Ala. Check any of these against the codon table on the previous tab — they all match.

1 · Nucleotide substitution

One base is swapped for another. Here, the 10th mRNA base changes from G to A:

mRNA
AUGAAGUUUAGCGCA
Protein
Met–Lys–Phe–Ser–Ala

The codon at that position changes from GGC (Gly) to AGC (Ser). Nothing else moves — every other triplet is exactly as it was, because a substitution replaces one letter without changing how many letters there are. The reading frame downstream is untouched.

2 · Nucleotide deletion

One base is removed outright — here, one of the U's inside the Phe codon:

mRNA
AUGAAGUUUGGCGCA
Protein
Met–Lys–Leu–Ala…

Removing one letter shifts every triplet boundary after it. The sequence still reads three letters at a time — it just now groups differently: UUG instead of UUU, and everything after that regrouped too. This is a frameshift mutation: not just the one position changes, but every codon downstream of the deletion point.

3 · Nucleotide insertion

One base is added — here, a G inserted a few positions in:

Protein
Met–Lys–Leu–Trp–Arg

Same mechanism as a deletion, opposite direction — adding a letter shifts the frame just as effectively as removing one. Three downstream codons come out different from the original, none of them by coincidence: the frame itself has moved.

The rule connecting all three

A codon is always read three letters at a time, starting from a fixed point. A substitution swaps a letter without changing the letter count, so only the one codon it falls inside is affected — everything else still lines up correctly. An insertion or deletion changes the letter count, so every codon boundary after that point shifts, and the entire downstream reading changes. The number of letters added or removed is what decides how much damage is done, not which letter it is.

Why doesn't inserting or deleting exactly 3 nucleotides cause a frameshift?

Because the frame is measured in units of three. Remove or add any multiple of 3 bases and every codon boundary after that point lands back in the same place it would have anyway — the triplet immediately at the change is altered (one amino acid gained, lost, or changed), but nothing downstream shifts. It's specifically indels not divisible by 3 that throw the frame off, which is exactly what happened above (1 base each time).

Is every substitution this disruptive?

No — a substitution can land three different ways. It can change the amino acid (a missense mutation, as above). It can create a premature stop codon partway through the protein (a nonsense mutation, which truncates everything after it). Or, thanks to degeneracy from the previous tab, the new codon can still code for the identical amino acid — a silent mutation, with no effect on the protein at all. Which of the three happens depends entirely on which codon the substitution lands in and which base within it changes.
